Apple Watch has a Readiness score now. What does a low one actually mean?

By Petter Höglander17 September 20269 min read

Apple Watch has a readiness score now. If you just got a Series 12 or an Ultra 4, you will wake up to a number from 0 to 10 and one of four words: Recover, Pace Yourself, Ready or Go For It.

So what does a low one mean? Short answer: last night your body looked less recovered than your own normal. Less sleep, lower HRV, a higher resting heart rate, a hard session in the legs, or a mix. It does not mean you are unhealthy, and it does not mean you cannot train. It means today is a day to find out why.

I built a recovery score myself before Apple did, so I have opinions about this. Here is how I would read it.

What Apple's Readiness score is made of

Readiness combines four things: your recent activity, your training load, your overnight vitals and your sleep. Apple announced it on 9 September 2026, and it arrives with the new watches on 18 September.

According to Apple's own support page, the score looks at:

  • Recent activity: active calories, workout effort and training load.
  • Overnight vitals: heart rate, respiratory rate, wrist temperature, HRV and blood oxygen.
  • Sleep: your recent sleep scores.

The four verdicts are fixed bands: 0 to 1 is Recover, 2 to 4 is Pace Yourself, 5 to 7 is Ready, 8 to 10 is Go For It. Apple says the algorithm was built with data from the Apple Heart and Movement Study, and that the new sensors measure HRV up to 24 times more often than before (Apple Newsroom).

A few details worth knowing:

  • It needs 7 nights of sleep tracking in the last 49 before you get a first score.
  • It moves during the day. Calories burned, naps and your daytime heart rate update it.
  • Apple has not published the weights. You know what goes in, not how much each part counts.
  • It only runs on Series 12 and Ultra 4. Not Series 11, not Ultra 3, not SE.

A low score is low compared to you, not to everyone

Every serious readiness or recovery score works the same way underneath. It learns your normal, then compares last night with it. A resting heart rate of 58 is great for one person and a warning for another. An HRV of 35 ms can be a good night or a bad one depending on whose wrist it is on.

That is also why you cannot compare your score with a friend's. Two people can both read "Pace Yourself" for completely different reasons, from completely different numbers.

Look at where the lines sit. On Oura, 65 is "Fair". On WHOOP, 65 is yellow, which WHOOP describes as your body maintaining. Apple's bottom band is only two numbers wide. The way I read that, Recover is saved for mornings that really are bad, and most ordinary tired mornings will land in Pace Yourself.

So if you switch from one device to another, don't read the new number with the old one's eyes. Give it two weeks and learn what your normal looks like on the new scale.

What usually pushes the score down

Almost always one of these:

  1. Sleep. Short, late, broken or at a different time than usual. In the score I built it is the biggest single input, about half.
  2. HRV below your baseline. Alcohol is the most predictable cause. Stress, a hard session and an infection that has not shown up yet are the others. I wrote a whole piece on why HRV goes down.
  3. Resting heart rate above your baseline. Often goes together with low HRV.
  4. Training load. A big session yesterday should lower today's score. That is not a problem, that is training.
  5. Temperature and breathing rate. When both drift up at the same time as your heart rate, your body is often fighting something.

The useful move is not to stare at the score. It is to look at which part moved. Your HRV, resting heart rate and sleep are all in the Health app, so open them.

What to actually do on a low day

Ask one question: was there a reason?

  • Yes (a hard session, drinks, a short night). Then the score is doing its job, it is just confirming what you already know. Do your day, keep the training easy or short, and fix the reason tonight.
  • No reason, and you feel fine. Train, but easy. See what tomorrow says. One low morning means very little.
  • No reason, you feel off, and resting heart rate or temperature is up. Take the rest day. If it is an infection, you will know by tomorrow, and you will be glad you did not do intervals.
  • Low for several mornings in a row. That is a trend, not a day. Take the whole week down a notch and look for the structural thing: sleep, workload, alcohol, stress.

And be realistic about what the score buys you. When researchers pooled eight studies comparing training guided by morning HRV with a fixed plan (199 people), the HRV-guided groups did not get dramatically fitter or faster: the effect on endurance performance was small and not statistically significant. What they did find was less likelihood of a negative response to training (Manresa-Rocamora et al., 2021). I think that is exactly the right expectation. A readiness score is not going to make you faster. It helps you have fewer bad weeks.

Don't let the watch overrule how you feel

This is the part I would underline. How you feel is data too, and good data.

A systematic review of 56 studies on athletes found that subjective measures, like mood, perceived stress and how recovered you feel, tracked training load more sensitively and more consistently than objective measures such as heart rate and blood markers, and that the two often did not even agree (Saw, Main & Gastin, British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2016).

So if the watch says Go For It and you feel wrecked, believe yourself. If it says Pace Yourself and you feel great, it is fine to train, just don't set a personal record that day to prove the watch wrong. The score is a second opinion, not the boss.

Apple says roughly the same thing, to its credit: Readiness does not account for existing conditions or medications, and it should not be the only thing you use to decide whether it is safe to exercise.

Apple's choice: a score that moves during the day

One design choice is worth pointing out, because it changes how you use it. Apple's score updates through the day as you burn calories, nap, or your daytime heart rate changes. That is closer to "how am I right now".

In Woah I did the opposite. The score is locked when the night's data arrives, so it is one stable answer for the day, and "Tank right now" is the separate thing that tracks how the day is draining you. I wanted the morning answer to be something you can plan around, not a number that changes its mind at lunch.

Neither is wrong. They answer different questions. Just know which one you are looking at, so a lower number at 3pm does not worry you.

My watch can't run Readiness. Am I missing something?

Less than the launch coverage suggests. The new sensor measures HRV more often, and I would expect that to make the score steadier. But the signals themselves, sleep, HRV, resting heart rate, breathing rate, workouts, are things older Apple Watches have been writing to Apple Health for years.

So you have three options:

  1. Do it by hand. Compare this morning's HRV and resting heart rate with your last 30 days in the Health app. It is slow, but it works.
  2. Use a recovery app that reads Apple Health and does the comparison for you. Woah is the one I built, and the score is free. I compared six of them, Woah included, in best recovery apps for Apple Watch.
  3. Upgrade. If you were going to anyway, Readiness is a real reason. I would not buy a watch for it alone.

Questions people ask

What does a low Readiness score on Apple Watch mean?

It means last night's signals, sleep, HRV, resting heart rate, temperature, breathing and your recent training load, looked less recovered than your own normal. Apple's bands are Recover (0 to 1) and Pace Yourself (2 to 4). Check which input moved before you decide what to do.

Which Apple Watch models have the Readiness score?

Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4, from 18 September 2026. Older models do not get it, because it depends on the new Health Sensing System. You also need at least 7 nights of sleep tracking in the last 49 before the first score.

Is Apple's Readiness score the same as a WHOOP recovery score?

Same idea, different scale. Readiness runs from 0 to 10 with four verdicts; WHOOP Recovery runs from 0 to 100 percent with red under 34 and green from 67. Both compare you with your own baseline, and neither number translates directly into the other.

Should I skip my workout if my readiness score is low?

Not automatically. If there was an obvious reason, like a hard session or a short night, train easy or short. If there was no reason and you feel off, or your resting heart rate is up, rest. In research, HRV-guided training mainly reduced bad responses to training rather than making people faster.

Can I get a readiness score on an older Apple Watch?

Not Apple's. But the inputs, sleep, HRV and resting heart rate, are already in Apple Health on older watches, so you can compare them with your 30-day average by hand or use an app that reads Apple Health and does it for you.
